РАЗБОР ПРИЛОЖЕННЫХ СЦЕНАРИЕВ
Открытие: конкретная сцена в первом предложении (гейт у шоссе, край заднего двора, частная дорога, дом за 200 тысяч при оценке 1,9 миллиона), потом цены домов и в третьем-пятом предложении скрытое противоречие, которое обещает ответ позже.
Удержание: каждые 150–250 слов новая цифра, дата или документ (цена лота, год постройки, номер правила, толщина стены), плюс «запомни этот закон, всё вокруг него вертится».
Переходы: через место и время («пройди дальше по улице», «зимой 2010 года», «в трёх кварталах»), а не через логические связки; новый блок почти всегда начинается с нового адреса или нового человека.
Средняя длина предложения: 14–18 слов, чередование коротких ударных фраз из 4–7 слов и длинных описаний до 30 слов.
Середина: один длинный реальный кейс с хронологией по датам (шторм 2 сентября, оползень, заседание комиссии) и живыми деталями (тонны камня, число футов, срок).
Финал: анафора «вы можете заплатить, вы можете построить, но вы не можете…», возвращение к сцене из открытия, одна афористичная мысль об океане, потом открытый вопрос в комментарии.

=== СЦЕНАРИЙ ===

On the western edge of San Francisco there is a four lane highway that the city is tearing out on purpose. It runs along Ocean Beach, past rows of pastel houses that sell for two, three, and sometimes four million dollars. The bulldozers are not coming because the road wore out. They are coming because the city decided the Pacific Ocean had already won the argument. So why would a city give up its own coastal road while the houses behind it keep getting more expensive?

The road opened in 1929. In the same years the city poured a concrete seawall along the beach and built a boardwalk lined with amusement rides. Back then the sand dunes still rolled unbroken from the beach almost to Golden Gate Park. The road was meant to be a scenic drive. Families took a Sunday car out to the water and watched the fog come in over the hood. For nearly a hundred years that is exactly what it was. Today the southern stretch is closed to cars behind orange barriers. The middle stretch has been turned into a park. And the sand keeps arriving on the pavement faster than the plows can push it back. The homeowners along the road did not vote for most of this. Yet the value of their houses is tied to a strip of asphalt that no longer has a future.

To understand how a city loses a highway, you have to start with the sand. Ocean Beach is roughly three and a half miles long. It is the widest beach in San Francisco and the only one that faces the open Pacific directly. The waves here are born in storms thousands of miles away. In winter they break at fifteen and twenty feet, and the surfers who paddle out are among the most experienced in the state. The wind blows onshore almost every afternoon. It carries fine dune sand east across the road in drifts that can close a lane by morning. City crews have plowed that sand for decades the way northern towns plow snow. Front loaders scoop it off the lanes and dump trucks carry it back to the beach. On the worst days the road closed entirely until the sand was cleared. Anyone who has driven the road in a westerly gale remembers the sound of grit against the windshield. That sand is the beach moving. It shifts north in summer and south in winter. Over the long run it has been leaving the southern end of the beach faster than it comes back.

The southern end is where the story of the road actually begins. South of Sloat Boulevard the highway does not sit behind a seawall. It sits on top of a sandy bluff about forty feet above the water. The bluff is made of old dune sand and decades of construction fill. Beneath it runs one of the most important pipes in the city. The Lake Merced Tunnel is a concrete sewer line fourteen feet across. It carries stormwater and wastewater from the west side of San Francisco to the Oceanside treatment plant beside the zoo. When it rains hard, that tunnel is what keeps sewage out of the ocean and off the streets of the Sunset District. The plant next to it handles a large share of the city's wastewater. So the question in front of the city was bigger than a road. It was about whether a forty foot pile of sand could keep protecting a tunnel, a plant, and a neighborhood behind them.

The winter that answered the question came in early 2010. A strong El Nino season sent one storm after another into the coast of central California. At Ocean Beach the waves cut straight into the bluff south of Sloat. In a matter of weeks the edge of the cliff moved back by roughly forty feet in places. Sections of the parking lots along the highway broke off and dropped onto the sand. The southbound lanes were closed as the pavement cracked within a few yards of the drop. City engineers stood at the edge and looked at the raw face of the bluff. The sewer tunnel was closer to daylight than anyone had planned for.

What happened next is the same move you see on every eroding coastline in California. Trucks brought in boulders. Thousands of tons of rock were pushed over the edge and stacked against the base of the bluff as an emergency revetment. Old concrete rubble that had been buried in the fill for decades tumbled out onto the beach. Chunks of curb and slab lay in the surf with rusted rebar sticking out of them. Surfers and dog walkers picked their way around the debris for years. The California Coastal Commission regulates almost everything built along the shoreline. It allowed the rock as a temporary measure. Temporary is a word that does a lot of work on this coast. Fifteen years later much of that rock is still there.

The rock protected the tunnel, but it did nothing for the beach in front of it. Along a hard revetment the sand tends to scour away. At high tide the water reaches the boulders with no dry sand left to walk on. The structure that saved the road was slowly erasing the beach the road was built to enjoy. For a city that sells itself on its coastline, that trade looked worse every winter. Photographs from the years after 2010 show tumbled rock and broken asphalt. A chain link fence stands where a parking lot used to be.

The storms did not stop after 2010 either. In January 2023 a string of atmospheric river storms hit the California coast for three weeks. At Sloat the waves once again reached the base of the bluff and pulled sand out from under the revetment. Crews came back with more rock and more trucks. Each repair bought another few winters. Nobody involved described it as a solution.

In 2012 a planning organization called SPUR published the Ocean Beach Master Plan. It was not a law, only a set of recommendations. But it laid out an idea that almost no American city had been willing to say out loud. South of Sloat, the plan said, the city should stop fighting. Remove the highway from that stretch. Reroute traffic onto Sloat and Skyline boulevards. Protect the sewer tunnel with a low wall buried under a restored beach instead of a pile of boulders standing in the surf. Planners call this managed retreat. Residents along the coast usually call it something less polite.

The city adopted the core of that idea. Under the name Ocean Beach Climate Change Adaptation Project, San Francisco committed to removing the roadway south of Sloat. The plan calls for a buried seawall to shield the Lake Merced Tunnel. New sand goes on top of it, and a coastal trail runs where the cars used to. It moved through years of environmental review and won its Coastal Commission approval in the early 2020s. Construction was expected to run through the middle of the decade. Cost estimates have shifted several times. I have not found a single final figure I would trust, but every version runs well past one hundred million dollars. The southern lanes were closed to traffic ahead of the demolition, and the barriers went up.

That was the part of the road the ocean took directly. The next part was taken by a ballot.

Between Sloat Boulevard and Lincoln Way, the upper highway runs about two miles behind the old 1929 seawall. Here the problem is sand piling up rather than sand disappearing, and the road itself was structurally fine. In the spring of 2020, during the pandemic, the city closed it to cars so people had somewhere to walk. Joggers, cyclists, and parents with strollers filled four lanes of empty asphalt. The closure was popular with families from across the city. It was deeply unpopular with many of the people who actually lived in the Sunset and used the road to get to work. For four years the city ran compromises. Cars on weekdays, pedestrians on weekends. Then in November 2024 the question went to every voter in San Francisco as Proposition K.

Proposition K asked whether the upper highway should be closed to private vehicles permanently and converted into a park. It passed with roughly fifty five percent of the vote. The map of that vote tells the whole story of the neighborhood. Precincts in the Sunset and Richmond districts, the neighborhoods closest to the beach, voted heavily against it. The eastern side of the city carried the measure. Those are residents who visit the beach on weekends and never sit in the commute traffic on Nineteenth Avenue. In April 2025 the city opened the stretch as Sunset Dunes. It is a linear park with painted murals on the asphalt, a skate spot, and benches facing the water. On a weekend afternoon thousands of people walk, roll, and ride along the old lanes. Food trucks park at the entrances. For the people who use it, the park is one of the best things the city has built in years.

For the people who live on the row, the picture is more complicated. Their driveways now open onto a park instead of a road. Deliveries, contractors, and moving trucks squeeze onto the narrow side streets behind the houses. Emergency vehicles have a longer route to the beach. And the plows that once kept the sand off the pavement come far less often, because there are no cars to protect.

The political bill came due five months later. Joel Engardio was the district supervisor who had championed the measure. In September 2025 he faced a recall election driven largely by residents angry about losing the road. He was removed from office. A supervisor recalled over a highway closure is rare anywhere in the country. It shows how much that strip of pavement meant to the people who lived along it. Traffic on the parallel streets, Sunset Boulevard and Nineteenth Avenue, became a daily argument at neighborhood meetings. Whatever you think of the outcome, the residents were right that something had been taken away from them.

Now walk the length of the road as it stands today and look at the houses. That is where the money is. The row of homes directly facing the highway was mostly built between the 1920s and the 1950s. They are two and three story stucco houses with garages on the ground floor and living rooms upstairs for the view. In the Outer Sunset the median home has been selling around one and a half to two million dollars in recent years. The oceanfront row along the highway commands more, and individual sales there have cleared three million dollars. Real estate listings describe the location with words like unobstructed and forever. The photographs are always taken on the rare clear afternoon, with the sun setting into the water and the fog held offshore. Buyers from the tech companies downtown have paid cash for these houses. Some of them moved in without ever spending a winter week on the block. On paper these are among the most desirable addresses in a very expensive city. In practice their owners deal with a set of problems that do not show up in listing photographs.

The first problem is sand. Every westerly wind moves the dunes in front of the seawall eastward. The road was the buffer that caught it. With the cars gone, the city no longer plows the upper highway the way it used to. The drifts creep across the old lanes toward the sidewalks and garage doors. Homeowners along the row sweep sand off their front steps the way other people sweep leaves. Salt spray pits the paint on cars parked outside. It corrodes window frames and door hinges within a few years. Fog sits over the neighborhood for much of the summer. The sunshine that the rest of California takes for granted often does not appear on this block until afternoon.

The second problem is water. Over the life of a thirty year mortgage it is the one that matters most. The 1929 seawall is aging. It was designed under Michael O'Shaughnessy, the city engineer who also built the Hetch Hetchy water system. His crews poured a curved concrete wall with a wide stairway running along its top. The wall was built to keep sand off a scenic road. It was never designed as a defense against a rising ocean, and it has been repaired, buried, and dug out many times since. Walk the beach at low tide and you can see where the concrete has been patched in different decades, each repair a slightly different color. California's official planning guidance tells cities to prepare for sea levels between roughly one and three feet higher by the end of this century. Higher numbers are considered possible. At Ocean Beach the beach already narrows to nothing during the largest winter swells. Waves reach the base of the wall and throw spray over the top onto the old lanes. Add more water and the same storms reach it more often. Nothing on the west side of the city is built to a higher standard than that wall.

California law already requires sellers to hand buyers a natural hazard disclosure before a sale closes. It lists flood zones, fire zones, and earthquake faults. Coastal erosion is a newer entry on that form, and it does not say how many winters a given seawall has left. Insurance follows the same pattern. A standard homeowner policy covers wind and fire, and flood coverage from the sea is a separate federal policy with its own limits. Several owners along the coast have learned that distinction only after the water arrived. On the Great Highway row, the house may be worth three million dollars. The coverage behind it is a fraction of that.

The third problem is written into the law. The Coastal Commission has become steadily less willing to approve new hard armoring anywhere in California. A wall that protects a house or a road destroys the public beach in front of it over time. The commission's job is the beach. San Francisco got its rock revetment south of Sloat only as an emergency. The price of that permission was a promise to remove the road and rebuild the beach. Suppose the owner of a three million dollar house on the highway wanted a new wall in front of the block. They would be asking the same agency that just told their own city to retreat. There is no realistic version of that request that ends with a permit.

Put those three problems together and you can see what the highway actually was for the people behind it. It was a buffer between their front doors and the ocean. A hundred feet of pavement and plowed sand absorbed whatever came off the water first. That buffer is now a park in the middle and a demolition site in the south. Nobody has proposed knocking down the houses, and nobody needs to. The city's own adaptation plan draws a line at the sewer tunnel and the treatment plant. The houses along the upper highway sit outside that line, behind a wall that the plan does not promise to replace. Ask a city engineer what happens to that wall in fifty years and the honest answer is that nobody has funded the study yet. The distance between the sea and the front door has simply gotten shorter. Every plan the city has published assumes it will keep getting shorter.

There is one more piece of the puzzle that most residents never see. Sand does not only leave Ocean Beach. Some of it is put back by machine. The Army Corps of Engineers regularly dredges the main shipping channel outside the Golden Gate to keep the port open. For years part of that dredged sand has been placed offshore of Ocean Beach or directly on the southern shore. Barges and pipelines move it, and bulldozers spread it along the bluff at Sloat. It is the same practice used on beaches in Florida and New Jersey. It buys time, and it has to be repeated. Every load of sand that arrives is a reminder. The beach in its natural state is no longer wide enough to protect what sits behind it.

To see how fast the ending can come, look at the neighbors who already lost. About fifteen miles south lies the town of Pacifica. A row of apartment buildings on Esplanade Avenue stood there on a bluff very much like the one at Sloat. In the winter of 2016 the same kind of storms that hit Ocean Beach in 2010 arrived again. They cut that bluff back until the balconies hung over open air. The city declared the buildings uninhabitable. Residents were given hours to carry out what they could. Excavators tore the structures down while news helicopters filmed from above. Those were ordinary rental apartments. Their owners had no more power to stop the ocean than anyone on the Great Highway does. Some of them had bought only a few years earlier at full market price. A bluff that looked solid on the day of the inspection was gone within a single winter. The difference at Ocean Beach is only that the sewer tunnel gave the city a reason to act before the buildings fell.

Go back further and this neighborhood has done all of this before. At the north end of the beach the road climbs toward the Cliff House. An amusement park called Playland at the Beach ran there for decades. It had a wooden roller coaster, a funhouse with a laughing mechanical woman at the door, and a carousel. It closed in 1972 and was replaced by condominiums. Just up the hill, the Sutro Baths were once the largest indoor swimming complex in the world. They burned in 1966 and were left as concrete ruins that fill with seawater at high tide. At the south end, the Fleishhacker Pool beside the zoo was for years the largest outdoor swimming pool in the United States. It was filled with water pumped straight from the ocean. It closed in 1971 after storms damaged the intake pipe, and the empty basin was eventually filled with dirt. Every generation at Ocean Beach has built something ambitious at the edge of the water. Every generation has then watched the edge move.

What is different this time is that the city wrote the retreat into an official plan and paid for it. That is the honest answer to the question this story started with. San Francisco is losing the highway to a single storm or a single election only on the surface. Underneath, it is giving the road up in stages. A sewer tunnel and a public beach were judged to be worth more than four lanes of asphalt. And the law will not allow anyone to hold the line with rock forever. The homeowners along the road are left with the view they paid for and a shrinking strip of sand between it and their living rooms.

You can buy a house on the Great Highway for three million dollars. You can stand at your upstairs window and watch the entire Pacific Ocean arrive in front of you every evening. You can sweep the sand off your steps every morning and repaint the salt off your garage door every few years. But you cannot get the city to plow the road that used to catch that sand, because the road is a park now. You cannot get the state to approve a wall, because the state just spent a decade convincing your own city to take one down. And you cannot make the beach stay put. The sand at Ocean Beach has been moving since before the seawall, before the highway, and before anyone drew a lot line in the dunes.

Which leaves the question that every owner on that row will eventually have to answer. If the city was right to pull its own highway back from the water, what does that say about the houses across the pavement? Tell me in the comments what you think San Francisco should do with the next hundred feet. Tell me whether you would sign a thirty year loan on a house that faces the open Pacific. I read every comment on these, and on this one I genuinely want to know where you land.
