On the twentieth of December, 1943, a German fighter pilot pulled up beside an American bomber over northern Germany and stayed there. He was close enough to see the American pilot's face through the cockpit glass. He had more than twenty victories already, and the bomber next to him was the easiest kill he would ever be offered. Its nose was gone. One engine was dead and two more were barely turning. Its tail guns did not move.

Inside that bomber were ten men on their very first combat mission. One of them was already dead. Four more were wounded, and their pilot, twenty-one years old, had no idea why the Messerschmitt on his wing had not fired.

What the German did next could have put him in front of a firing squad. Both men kept it quiet for almost half a century, and neither of them knew the other's name until 1990.

This is what happened to the ten men of a B-17 called Ye Olde Pub, seat by seat, minute by minute, from the briefing room in England to a phone call forty-six years later. It is also the story of the one man outside the aircraft who decided whether any of them came home, and of the parts of that story the records still cannot settle.

A B-17F Flying Fortress was a long, narrow aluminium tube with four engines and a machine gun pointing out of almost every opening, and on this mission it carried ten men. To understand what happened to each of them, you need to know where each one sat, which way he faced, and how far he was from a way out.

Right at the front, in the Plexiglas nose, sat two officers. Second Lieutenant Albert Sadok, the navigator, was called Doc. Next to him was Second Lieutenant Robert Andrews, Andy, the bombardier. Nothing but a sheet of clear plastic stood between them and everything coming at the formation head on.

Just behind and above them, on the flight deck, sat the pilot, Second Lieutenant Charles Brown, Charlie, a farm boy from Weston, West Virginia, the youngest of six children. In the right-hand seat was his copilot, Second Lieutenant Spencer Luke, who everyone called Pinky. Directly behind the two pilots stood Sergeant Bertrand Coulombe, Frenchy, the flight engineer, who also manned the top turret, two guns that could swing all the way around above the fuselage. Remember that turret. It matters later.

Behind the bomb bay was the radio room, where Sergeant Richard Pechout worked the set. Below the floor, folded into a steel ball hanging under the belly, was Sergeant Samuel Blackford, Blackie, the ball turret gunner. Further back, in the open waist, two men stood facing out of opposite windows. Sergeant Lloyd Jennings on the left, and on the right Sergeant Alex Yelesanko, at twenty-nine the oldest man on board, called Russian by the crew.

And at the very end of the aircraft, past the tail wheel, kneeling in a cramped position under the rudder and facing backwards, was Sergeant Hugh Eckenrode, Ecky, the tail gunner.

Every one of them shared the same air, and up there it was barely air at all. Above about ten thousand feet a man needs oxygen to stay conscious, so each of them breathed through a rubber mask fed by the aircraft's oxygen system. The cold was worse. The waist windows were open to the sky, and at bombing altitude the wind coming through them could freeze skin in seconds. A bare hand on the metal could stick to it. The gunners wore electrically heated suits plugged into the aircraft, and the suits only worked as long as the aircraft's electrics did. Keep that in mind too, because on this mission the oxygen, the heat and the electrics would all fail together.

Ten men, spread from the nose to the tail, and on this day only one of them would never leave his seat.

They belonged to the 527th Bomb Squadron of the 379th Bomb Group, flying out of RAF Kimbolton in England. The target that morning was Bremen, and specifically a factory that built Focke-Wulf 190 fighters. At the briefing they were told to expect hundreds of German fighters. Bremen itself was defended by more than two hundred and fifty anti-aircraft guns.

Brown's crew was first given a slot the bomber crews called Purple Heart Corner, a position on the outer edge of the formation. German fighters liked the edges, because a bomber out there had fewer guns around it to help. It was the kind of place a new crew got put. Then three bombers turned back with mechanical problems, and Brown was ordered to move up to the front of the formation instead. It looked like luck. It did not stay that way.

The bomb run began at twenty-seven thousand, three hundred feet. The air outside was seventy-six degrees below zero, and the run lasted ten minutes, ten minutes in which the bomber had to fly straight and level while the guns below tried to find it.

They found it before the bombs were away. Flak shattered the Plexiglas nose, the exact spot where Doc Sadok and Andy Andrews were sitting. Number two engine was knocked out. Number four, which was already giving trouble before the mission, was hit again and had to be throttled back so it would not overspeed and tear itself apart. Brown kept the aircraft on the run, and the bombs went down on the target. But a B-17 with one dead engine and one crippled one cannot hold formation speed. Slowly, the rest of the group pulled ahead.

That is when Ye Olde Pub became what every bomber crew feared most. A straggler.

A formation of B-17s was a wall of guns, dozens of bombers covering each other. A single bomber falling behind was alone, slow and easy to find, and the German fighters went after it at once. More than a dozen of them, Messerschmitt 109s and Focke-Wulf 190s, came in from every direction, and they kept coming for more than ten minutes.

By the time it was over, the list of damage read like a list of everything a bomber needs to stay alive. Number three engine was hit and running at half power. With one engine dead and two damaged, the aircraft had, at best, about forty percent of its power left. The oxygen system was damaged. So were the hydraulics and the electrics. Half the rudder was gone, and so was the left elevator.

And then the guns stopped. With the electrical systems shot through, the heating died, and at those temperatures the firing mechanisms froze. Of the eleven guns the gunners had to fight with, only three were still working. Two of them were in Frenchy Coulombe's top turret, and one in the nose.

Now go back through the aircraft, seat by seat.

In the tail, Ecky Eckenrode had been hit directly by a cannon shell. He was killed instantly. In the right waist, Alex Yelesanko had been badly wounded in the leg by shrapnel. In the radio room, Dick Pechout had been hit in the eye. Blackie Blackford, in the ball turret, had a different problem. The electrical heating wires in his flying suit had shorted out, and his feet were freezing. Charlie Brown himself was wounded in the right shoulder.

And with the oxygen system shot up, Brown passed out at the controls. Nobody could say afterwards exactly how long he was out. When he came to, the bomber was far lower than it had been, low enough to breathe without a mask, and still flying. Accounts differ on how far it fell and who held it in the air while he was unconscious. What they agree on is that it was now down near the ground, alone, deep inside Germany.

The crew carried morphine for exactly this situation, small syrettes meant to be pushed straight into a wounded man. They were frozen solid. The radio was destroyed.

The men talked about bailing out. Over Germany, that meant capture, but it meant living. Then they looked at Yelesanko. With his leg torn up, he could not be expected to survive a parachute landing, and nobody was willing to jump and leave him alone in the aircraft. So they stayed. A bomber that could barely fly, with three guns, one dead man and four wounded, was going to try to cross Germany and the North Sea with the whole crew on board.

At an airfield below, a Luftwaffe pilot named Franz Stigler was waiting for his Messerschmitt 109 to be refuelled and rearmed. He was twenty-eight, from Bavaria, and he had been flying since he was a boy, first gliders at twelve, then airliners for Lufthansa before the war. He had fought in North Africa and over Sicily. By some accounts he had already shot down two B-17s that same day. His own aircraft was not in good shape either. An American point-five-oh bullet was lodged in its radiator, which meant the engine could overheat at any time.

Then he saw it. A lone B-17, low and slow, passing near the field. Stigler took off and closed in quickly.

Every bomber he brought down counted toward the Knight's Cross, the medal every fighter pilot in Germany wanted. A crippled B-17 with no escort was about as safe a target as the war ever offered. He lined up behind it and waited for the tail gunner to fire.

The tail guns never moved.

So he came closer. Through the holes that flak and cannon fire had torn in the fuselage, he could see inside. He saw the tail gunner, and he saw the blood. He saw wounded men, and others trying to help them. He saw the bare metal where the nose should have been.

And he remembered something his commander in North Africa, Gustav Rödel, had told his pilots. If he ever saw or heard of one of them shooting at a man in a parachute, he would shoot that pilot himself.

Years later, Stigler explained what went through his mind. To him, it was just like they were in a parachute. He saw them, and he could not shoot them down.

He held his fire. In Nazi Germany, a pilot letting an enemy bomber escape on purpose was not a breach of etiquette. It was treason, and it could be punished with death. Stigler knew that. He did it anyway.

On the flight deck, Charlie Brown looked out and saw a German fighter sitting right next to his wing. He could not understand why the German was not firing, and a fighter that close and that quiet could only mean something he had not seen yet.

Stigler tried to tell him what to do. Twice he gestured and mouthed through the canopy. Land, he meant. Land in Germany and surrender, and your wounded men will get a doctor. Or turn north. Sweden was neutral and close, and the crew would sit out the war there, alive.

Brown did not understand. The crew did not understand. The bomber kept flying west, toward the coast.

So Stigler did the only other thing he could. He moved into formation beside the B-17, on its left wing, and stayed there. Any German anti-aircraft battery on the coast that looked up would see a German fighter flying with the bomber, and would hold its fire. For several minutes, the most dangerous thing in the sky over Ye Olde Pub was protecting it.

Brown still did not trust him. He ordered Frenchy Coulombe in the top turret to aim his guns at the Messerschmitt, but not to open fire. It was a warning. Get away from us.

By then, they were over open water. Stigler understood the message, and he understood something else, that the bomber was finally out of German airspace and he could do nothing more. He looked across at Brown one last time, raised his hand in a salute, and turned away.

That was the last either man saw of the other for forty-six years.

Ye Olde Pub still had about two hundred and fifty miles of the North Sea to cross. In December, a man who went into that water had minutes. The aircraft was on partial power with a broken tail, a dead radio and no oxygen, carrying a dead man and four wounded, and it was still losing height.

It made it. Brown brought the B-17 over the English coast and put it down at RAF Seething, the base of a different bomb group, the 448th, because it would not have got much farther.

Here is what happened to each of the ten.

Hugh Eckenrode, the tail gunner, was the only man killed. He died on the twentieth of December, 1943, on his first mission.

The other nine survived. Yelesanko, the man they would not leave behind, lived because they did not. Pechout, Blackford and Brown recovered from their wounds. Doc Sadok and Andy Andrews, who had been sitting in a nose that no longer existed, came home. Pinky Luke, Frenchy Coulombe, Lloyd Jennings, all of them walked away from that aircraft.

Ye Olde Pub never flew in combat again. It was sent back to the United States in April 1944 and scrapped in August 1945.

At the debriefing, Brown told his officers that a German fighter pilot had let them go. The story did not leave the room. The decision was made to keep it quiet, and for decades almost nobody heard it.

Brown went back up and completed his combat tour. Stigler kept flying for Germany. By the end of the war, he was flying the Messerschmitt 262 jet fighter in one of the last Luftwaffe units still in the air. He never talked about the B-17. He could not have, not while the war lasted. After the war, his logbook was confiscated and never returned.

That is where the story should have ended. Two men who never learned each other's names, on opposite sides of an ocean.

Charlie Brown went home to West Virginia and went to college. He came back to the new United States Air Force in 1949, served until 1965, then worked for the State Department, and in 1972 retired to Miami to become an inventor. Franz Stigler moved to Canada in 1953 and built a successful business.

In 1986, Brown was invited to speak at a reunion of combat pilots in Alabama. Someone asked whether he had any memorable missions. He thought for a moment and told them about the German who escorted his bomber out and saluted.

After that, he could not let it go. For four years he searched American and German air force records for the unknown pilot, and found almost nothing. Finally, he wrote a letter to a newsletter for former fighter pilots.

A few months later, a letter arrived from Canada. It said, I was the one.

When the two men spoke on the phone, Stigler described his aircraft, the escort and the salute. Every detail matched what Brown had carried for forty-six years. In 1990, they met face to face.

They became close friends, and they stayed that way for the rest of their lives. Franz Stigler died in March 2008. Charlie Brown died eight months later, in November.

In 2008, at Brown's request, the men of his crew were each awarded the Silver Star. Brown himself received the Air Force Cross for keeping that aircraft on the bomb run and bringing it home. The last surviving member of the crew, Lloyd Jennings, the left waist gunner, died in 2016.

Before we finish, it is worth being honest about what the records do not say.

Almost everything we know about the escort comes from two men, telling it nearly fifty years later. There was no German report, because Stigler could not file one. There was no American one either, because the story was kept quiet. So some details do not line up. Brown remembered the fighter appearing off his right wing. Other versions put Stigler on the left, where he stayed for the escort. Stigler's total is also uncertain. Researchers working in the German archives found records for twenty-six of his victories, plus one unconfirmed, and with his logbook gone, nobody can say for sure how many there really were. Stigler himself said that after that day, he lost his appetite for chasing the Knight's Cross.

What nobody disputes is the outcome. A German pilot had a defenceless enemy bomber in front of his guns, and he let it go.

So here is the question. Put yourself in that cockpit over Germany, with no engines to spare, a German fighter on your wing, and a hand pointing you toward a German airfield or toward Sweden. Do you take the offer and keep your wounded crew alive for certain, or do you turn for the sea and the long way home? Brown chose the sea, and it was the right choice.
